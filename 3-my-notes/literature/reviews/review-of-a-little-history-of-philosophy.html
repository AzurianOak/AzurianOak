"A Little History of Philosophy"

Basic Info

Price: £9.99
Author: Nigel Warburton (PhD)
Date of publication: 11 Sept. 2012
Publisher: Yale University Press
Rating: 8/10

General Outline

The book is a concise documenting of most major philosophers in the western tradition; it starts with Plato & Socrates in Ancient Greece and leads into figures like Pyrrho, Locke, Machiavelli, Hobbes, Mill, Hume, Kant, Hegel, Nietzsche, Russell, Wittgenstein, John Rawls and ends with Peter Singer. Other figures covered include Darwin, Marx, Freud & Ayer. For every philosopher covered Warburton gives a brief outline of their major beliefs. This could be their ethics, metaphysics, theology or even their specific branch of a school of philosophy. For example, Warburton goes into basic detail regarding utilitarianism with regards to Bentham & Mill.

Important or otherwise interesting titbits of history that are in relation to the philosopher are also abundant. The one that sticks out in my mind most is the one about Schopenhauer and ethics. Whilst Schopenhauer was a moralist (As he believed that we are all one under the Will), and believed that any negative action on someone else was in turn a negative action on ourselves (This just sounds like the golden rule but with metaphysics, although any moral system sounds this way to me), he once pushed an elderly woman down a flight of stairs. Showing no remorse for the rest of his life for her consequent pain, he wrote: "obit anus, abit onus"; this is Latin for: "the old woman dies, the burden goes.". He wrote this on the woman's death certificate. Not very moral - at least in the eyes of Schopenhauerian ethics. Warburton cites this as proof of Schopenhauer's hypocrisy in terms of his philosophy. This memorable rebuttal to the philosophy of Schopenhauer has kept with me weeks after I finished this book.

Before I read this book, I considered myself a fan of philosophy. Indeed, I bought the book after vising the University of Lancaster on their open day. I read the chapter on Plato & Socrates alongside their chapter on Pyrrho. Before doing this, I really had no understanding of philosophy. In my years as a teenager I had attempted to "read" first Nietzsche and Schopenhauer. All this truly amounted to was cheap copies of their books on my desk never being understood. Anyone with a basic understanding of philosophy - I'm sure - isn't shocked to hear such! As a result my only (limited) knowledge on philosophy was from YouTube videos. Some dubious and some not. I had mainly watched videos regarding Nietzsche (I say this, it is unlikely I watched more than 10 in a multi-year period). I do continue to remember one about Hume's ethics however.

This is to say, I was a fan of philosophy. Not a student. Ironic given I had just left the subject talk on the Lancaster open day regarding philosophy! This changed with Warburton's book. Before I thought Nietzschean ethics was interesting; thinking that topics like metaphysics was beyond any reason to be worth researching; having no knowledge of the depth of certain topics such as Utilitarianism; etc. I think the photo I took of this book highlights how interesting I found it.

You can't even see how many post-it notes or tabs I have in the book.

Positives

To continue singing the praises of this book: it is fun. It doesn't feel like you're trudging through the Critique of Pure Reason by Kant. The book feels alive, virile and ready for conversation. It does a good job, I feel, of letting me know the major views of major philosophers since Ancient Greece. Virtually every major idea has a well grounded and easy to approach conceit ("a clever or surprising comparison" - Cambridge Dictionary website) attached. This makes such complex ideas such as Schopenhauer's Will or easier topics such as Kant's synthetic (knowledge through experience; "all cats have tails" is false as not all cats have tails which we know by looking at certain cats, is an example by Warburton) and analytic (true by definition; "all men are male", as one example by Warburton states) arguments.

The artwork also adds a nice flair and element of eye candy which virtually every philosophy book would benefit from having. The margins of this book are also comfortable enough to allow for annotating in the book alongside enough room for the adhesive of post-it notes and tabs to be used. The general build quality is superb given it's a paperback for £9.99. It survived well in both the confines of my bag and blazer pocket.

Negatives

Not all philosophers receive a counter-argument. This is an important flaw given this book acts as an introductory for many people who, likely, lack critical thinking. Somewhat related is the lack of detail sometimes. Most ideas only get a paragraph or two. I don't recall one getting an entire page. Certain key words - such as "a priori" - aren't clearly defined in the book as well. Leading to me having to look up a definition. Given the sleigh of resources online this isn't an issue but does go against the heart of this book.

I also feel that certain key philosophers are left out. Most notably: Foucault. Although he book does cover Sartre, it seems skips completely on Foucault. Even though, as it is widely regarded, Foucault is one of the most important figures in 20th century philosophy. My bias is relevant given I like Foucault though. Another flaw, although this is a nit-pick, is the book claims to cover Camus; the post-WW2 French Nietzschean. From what I recall he receives not even a page in a chapter about Sartre.

Conclusion

Whilst not a substitute for a work such as "A New History of Western Philosophy" by Anthony Kenny or Russell's famous work on the history of philosophy, this book provides a firm starting point to understand philosophy as a whole. Personally it was of immense use in helping me find philosophers I may have knew (or not have ever heard of) and wanting to learn more about them. Figures that stand out to me in this regard are Pyrrho, Machiavelli, Bentham, Mill, etc. This book also motivated me to take studying philosophy at home more seriously as I am now working through "A Brief History of Thought" by Luc Ferry and after it will begin reading "Philosophy: The Basics" (another book by Warburton). Hopefully I will also finish Kenny's work on the history of western philosophy by the end of the year as well.

Over all, I think this is a 8/10 book that anyone with a fledging interest in philosophy or who is about to enter an undergrad in philosophy should read. It most certainly awoke a spirit in me that will continue to drive me to study philosophy.
