Review

Tasmin Spargo’s “Foucault and Queer Theory” is a sub-100 page booklet which summarises Foucault’s relation to queer theory in a concise – although not detailed – manner. As an introductory book to the this subject matter, however, this may be a positive. I wouldn’t recommend it for those wishing to understand Foucault in terms of a greater level nonetheless.

Michel Foucault (p.g., 45 of "The History of Sexuality: The Will to Knowledge", Vintage Books):

It [homosexuality] was everywhere present in him: at the root of all his actions [...] The sodomite had been a temporary aberration; the homosexual was now a species.

One thing this booklet did outstanding in was explaining why Foucault didn’t regard homosexuality as an entirely biological condition, with Foucault taking the historian approach that “homosexual” is a modern term. Foucault, and as a result so does Tasmin Spargo, juxtaposes the sodomite with the homosexual. This is because homosexuality is a socially constructed category of knowledge, instead of a biological fact. In my personal opinion, this depends on our definition of “homosexual”. If “homosexual” is used in the biological definition, that is AMAB who has sexual relations with another AMAB and/or feels attraction to another AMAB (In the medical field this is dubbed MSM I believe), then it is completely mugwump of any social factors. For example, when I say “Shakespeare was homosexual”, I mean that Shakespeare had same-sex desire. I do not mean he was listening to Lady Gaga while wearing a crop top shouting “SLAY” at the actors. Due to this, I feel the term “gay” may be more accurate as a species, although the medical origins of the word “homosexual” itself cannot be ignored.

Tasmin Spargo, p.g., 18 of “Foucault and Queer Theory”

So while 16th century men and women might be urged to confess that they had indulged in shameful sexual practices against the law of God and the land, the late 19th century man engaging in a sexual relationship with another man would be seen, and be encouraged to see himself, as ‘homosexual’.”

One of main reasons for this differentiation is the fact it became an identity, therefore a species that could be studied, and notably, attacked. However, a reverse discourse began following the 1960s, causing homosexuals to begin identifying as “gay”, using the same medical terms once used against them to fight for themselves.

Tasmin Spargo, p.g., 20 of “Foucault and Queer Theory”

The fact that a subject position or identity is constructed does not make it any less real for the identified. The homosexual was pathologised as a perverse or deviant type, a case of arrested development, a suitable case for treatment, in short as an aberration from a heterosexual norm. As such, he was subject to the disciplining, marginalising and subordinating effects of social control.

Tasmin Spargo, p.g., 28 of “Foucault and Queer Theory”

”Being gay or lesbian was a matter of pride, not of pathology; of resistance, not of self-effacement. […] gay liberation contested the representation of same-sex desires and relationships as unnatural, deviant or incomplete.”

One interesting idea posited from the book is the idea of the “closet” inherently imposing hetero-supremacism over gays & lesbians, as it implies being gay is the abnormality – as opposed to heterosexuality. I have to admit, I never viewed it this way and it makes me question why I personally put so much emphasis on “coming out”.

Tasmin Spargo, p.g., 30 of “Foucault and Queer Theory”

For lesbians and gay men, being ‘out’ or ‘in the closet’ became a crucial marker of their sexual politics. Coming out suggested emerging from confinement and concealment into the open, a movement from secrecy to public affirmation.

Positives

→ Briefly explains the origins and what/why of queer theory
→ Short! I read this within 3 continuous hours
→ Presents interesting ideas relating to queer theory, such as Foucault’s “species” or Derrida’s “supplement”

Negatives

→ Doesn’t relate things to Foucault as would be expected given the title of “Foucault and Queer Theory”
→ Doesn’t apply Foucauldian ideas to queer theory that often
→ Rarely in-depth

Conclusion

Although short, this booklet does present some interesting ideas that will be new to many “queer” or “gay” people that they, likely, haven’t considered before. Given it can easily be read in one sitting, alongside the fact it is cheap to buy, I’d surely recommend it to people – just not if they wish to understand Foucault.
